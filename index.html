The Game

What Is Cricket?

Cricket is a team sport played between two sides, usually of eleven players each. One team bats and tries to score runs, while the other team bowls and fields, trying to get the batters out and limit the runs. After an agreed number of overs or when enough batters are out, the teams swap roles.

The side that scores more runs in total wins. Cricket began in England and spread through many countries, becoming especially popular in South Asia, Australia, the Caribbean, Africa and the United Kingdom. In India and nearby countries it is played everywhere, from stadiums to streets and village grounds.

At its heart, cricket is a contest between bat and ball, played with strict fairness and a strong tradition of respect.

The Ground

The Field and Fielding Positions

A cricket ground is usually oval, with a boundary rope marking the edge. In the centre lies the pitch, a flat strip about 22 yards (20.12 metres) long, with a set of three stumps and two bails at each end.

Main Roles

  • Batters: two batters are on the field at a time, one facing the bowler and one at the other end.
  • Bowler: delivers the ball, six legal deliveries making one over.
  • Wicketkeeper: stands behind the stumps and catches or stumps.
  • Fielders: placed around the ground to stop runs and take catches.
  • Umpires: apply the Laws of the game and make decisions.

Keeping Score

How Scoring and Getting Out Work

Runs

Batters score by hitting the ball and running between the stumps. Each completed run is worth one.

Boundaries

A ball that reaches the rope along the ground scores four; if it clears the rope without bouncing, it scores six.

Extras

Wides, no-balls, byes and leg-byes add runs to the batting team without the batter hitting the ball.

Wickets

A batter is out when dismissed. Ten dismissals usually end an innings.

Common Ways to Be Out

  • Bowled: the ball hits the stumps and removes a bail.
  • Caught: a fielder catches the ball before it bounces.
  • LBW (leg before wicket): the ball would have hit the stumps but strikes the batter's body, subject to conditions in the Laws.
  • Run out: the stumps are broken while the batter is out of the crease.
  • Stumped: the wicketkeeper breaks the stumps while the batter is out of the crease.

The exact Laws are maintained by the sport's official lawmakers, and competitions may add playing conditions. Always check the current Laws and local rules for details.

Kit

Cricket Equipment

  • Bat: traditionally made of willow, with a flat face for hitting the ball.
  • Ball: hard cork-and-leather ball in formal cricket; softer or tennis balls are common in casual play.
  • Stumps and bails: three upright stumps topped by two small bails at each end of the pitch.
  • Helmet: protects the head, especially against fast bowling.
  • Pads and gloves: protect the legs and hands of the batters and wicketkeeper.
  • Box (abdominal guard): essential protective equipment for male players.
  • Clothing and shoes: comfortable whites or coloured kit and shoes with good grip.

Beginners and children should use equipment sized for their age, and should start with a softer ball and proper protection under supervision.

Learn to Play

Basic Skills for Beginners

Batting

Start with a balanced stance, watch the ball closely and play with a straight bat. Learn to defend before trying big shots.

Bowling

Focus on a smooth run-up and a repeatable action. Accuracy matters more than speed when starting out.

Fielding

Stay alert, keep your eyes on the ball and practise catching and throwing with a partner.

Running Between Wickets

Good calling and communication with your partner prevent run outs and save runs.

Wicketkeeping

Needs sharp reflexes, quick hands and good footwork. Best learned with a coach and the right gloves.

Captaincy and Teamwork

Reading the game, changing bowlers and encouraging teammates are skills that develop with experience.

Fitness and Safety

Cricket needs a mix of stamina, strength, flexibility and good reflexes. Warm up before playing, stay hydrated in hot weather and use sun protection during long matches. Bowlers in particular should build up workload gradually to avoid overuse injuries. Any head or neck injury must be taken seriously and examined by a medical professional.

Spirit of the Game

Cricket places great importance on fair play. Players are expected to respect umpires, opponents and teammates, accept decisions graciously and play within both the Laws and the spirit of the game.

Quick Reference

Cricket Glossary

Over: a set of six legal deliveries bowled from one end.
Innings: a team's turn to bat.
Crease: the marked lines near the stumps that define a batter's safe area.
Maiden: an over in which no runs are scored off the bat.
Duck: a batter dismissed without scoring.
Century: a score of 100 runs or more by a batter.
Wide: a ball bowled too far from the batter to hit; adds an extra run.
No-ball: an illegal delivery, such as overstepping, giving an extra run.
Spin: bowling in which the ball is made to turn after pitching.
Pace: fast bowling.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the easiest way to start playing cricket?

Begin with a soft ball and simple rules in a park or school ground, then join a local club or school team where a coach can teach basic skills safely.

Why can a Test match last five days?

Test cricket is designed to test patience, skill and endurance. Each team can bat twice with no limit on overs, so a match needs more time to reach a result.

Can girls and women play cricket?

Yes. Women's and girls' cricket is played at school, club and international level in many countries.

Where can I find the official Laws of cricket?

The Laws are published by the sport's official lawmaking body and by national governing bodies. Check their official websites for the latest version.
